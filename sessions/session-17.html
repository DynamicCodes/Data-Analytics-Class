Simulation

Experimenting on a model instead of the real world

Simulation Imitating a real-world system or process with a mathematical or computational model to study its behavior under different scenarios. It predicts outcomes, performance and variability without physical experiments, which is valuable when real-life testing is too costly, risky or slow.
Risk analysis Identifying, assessing and quantifying the uncertainties that could affect a project or model. It asks: what could go wrong, how likely is it, and what would the impact be? Examples include financial risk in investment portfolios and uncertainty in demand forecasts.

The two work together. Risk analysis defines the uncertainties, such as fluctuating costs or variable demand; simulation models how those uncertainties affect results. Together they let decision-makers quantify uncertainty, visualize possible outcomes, and choose strategies that hold up under risk.

System dynamics: feedback loops

System dynamics models how quantities (stocks) change through flows that depend on each other. In an epidemic, more infected people cause more new infections (a reinforcing loop), while fewer susceptible people slow the spread (a balancing loop). The classic SIR model tracks Susceptible, Infected and Recovered people.

Monte Carlo and risk metrics

The core of simulation-based risk analysis

  1. Assign distributions

    Give each uncertain input a probability distribution.

  2. Sample randomly

    Draw one random value for every input.

  3. Repeat thousands of times

    Calculate the outcome for each set of draws.

  4. Aggregate

    Summarize the outcomes as a probability distribution.

The risk analysis process

From problem to decision in six steps

  1. Define the problem

    Objectives, variables and constraints.

  2. Develop the model

    A mathematical or computational representation, such as Profit = Revenue − Cost.

  3. Identify uncertainties

    Represent uncertain variables with distributions: Demand ~ Normal(μ, σ), Price ~ Triangular(min, mode, max).

  4. Run simulations

    Thousands of Monte Carlo iterations.

  5. Analyze results

    Output distributions, mean, variance, percentiles, confidence intervals.

  6. Decide

    Choose strategies that minimize risk or maximize expected return.

Advantages

Handles complex, nonlinear systems; quantifies uncertainty; improves decisions; enables scenario and sensitivity testing; cheaper than real-world trials.

Limitations

Needs accurate data and assumptions; can be computationally intensive; wrong probability models lead to wrong conclusions; it measures uncertainty but doesn't remove it.

Simulation and risk analysis give a better understanding of uncertainty, quantified risk, and data-driven planning. They are essential to predictive and prescriptive analytics in finance, supply chains, healthcare and AI decision systems.

Linear optimization

Straight-line objectives and constraints

Maximize or minimize Z = c₁x₁ + c₂x₂ + … + cₙxₙ   subject to   a₁₁x₁ + a₁₂x₂ + … + a₁ₙxₙ ≤ b₁, …,   xᵢ ≥ 0

With two variables you can solve it graphically: each constraint cuts off part of the plane, leaving a feasible region, and the optimum lies at one of its corner points.

The slide's LP, solved graphically

Interactive

Maximize Z = 3x₁ + 5x₂ subject to 2x₁ + x₂ ≤ 100 and x₁ + 3x₂ ≤ 90. Slide the iso-profit line outward: the last feasible point it touches is the optimum. Change the objective coefficients to see the optimum jump between corners.

The slides give the solution as x₁ = 30, x₂ = 20 with Z = 190. That point is feasible, but it isn't a corner of the region and it isn't optimal: the true optimum is the corner x₁ = 42, x₂ = 16, where both constraints are binding, with Z = 3 × 42 + 5 × 16 = 206.

Nonlinear optimization

Curves, local optima and iterative search

min or max f(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ)   subject to   gᵢ(x) ≤ 0,   hⱼ(x) = 0,   with f, g or h nonlinear

The objective or constraints may involve quadratic, exponential, logarithmic or trigonometric terms. Unlike LP, the solution may not lie on the boundary, and there may be several local minima or maxima.

Example: Lagrange multipliers

Minimize f(x₁, x₂) = x₁² + 3x₂² subject to x₁ + x₂ = 10. Form the Lagrangian L = x₁² + 3x₂² + λ(10 − x₁ − x₂) and set its derivatives to zero:

∂L/∂x₁ = 2x₁ − λ = 0,   ∂L/∂x₂ = 6x₂ − λ = 0,   x₁ + x₂ = 10  ⇒  x₁ = 3x₂  ⇒  x₁ = 7.5, x₂ = 2.5

See why the Lagrange solution works

Interactive

The ellipses are contours of f: every point on one ellipse has the same value. Slide along the constraint line x₁ + x₂ = 10. The minimum is where the line just touches a contour, so the gradient of f points the same way as the constraint's gradient.

Linear: strengths and limits

Easy to interpret and compute, with guaranteed global optimality. But it assumes proportionality and additivity and can't capture curved effects.

Nonlinear: strengths and limits

Models real-world complexity: curvature, thresholds, diminishing returns. But it may stop at a local optimum, needs good starting points, and is computationally expensive.

In modern analytics

AI and machine learning (loss minimization, hyperparameter tuning), operations research (supply chain and logistics), econometrics (parameter estimation), and prescriptive analytics (recommending the best actions).

Session 17 in summary

  • Simulation imitates a system to study its behavior; risk analysis defines the uncertainties it explores.
  • Monte Carlo, discrete-event, system dynamics and agent-based simulations suit different kinds of systems.
  • Monte Carlo turns uncertain inputs into a distribution of outcomes, summarized by the mean, percentiles, VaR and CVaR; sensitivity analysis shows which input matters most.
  • Linear programs have their optimum at a corner of the feasible region and are solved by the graphical or simplex method.
  • Nonlinear problems can have optima inside the region and multiple local optima; Lagrange multipliers handle equality constraints, and gradient methods search iteratively.
