Supervised segmentation

From discovering relationships to grouping by predicted outcome

Predictive modelling uses historical data and statistical or machine learning methods to predict future outcomes. The journey from correlation to supervised segmentation is the move from discovering relationships in data to grouping or classifying entities by predictive patterns.

Correlation

Measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship and shows which features relate to the target, but doesn't establish causation.

Prediction

Quantifies and uses those relationships: select relevant features, build models to estimate or classify outcomes, and interpret the results as business insights.

Supervised segmentationDivide entities into data-driven groupsSegments such as low, medium and high risk
Supervised segmentation Dividing a population into distinct, meaningful groups based on outcomes predicted by a supervised model. Unlike unsupervised clustering, which groups data without labels, it is guided by a known target variable such as churn = Yes/No. It uses labeled training data, defines segments by rules, probabilities or thresholds from the model, and helps explain why outcomes occur.

Conceptual workflow

  1. Data understanding

    Identify input features and the outcome variable.

  2. Correlation analysis

    Understand relationships and dependencies.

  3. Feature selection

    Keep only statistically significant predictors.

  4. Model building

    Develop a supervised regression or classification model.

  5. Prediction and segmentation

    Use predicted values or probabilities to form distinct segments.

  6. Validation and interpretation

    Evaluate performance and the meaning of each segment.

Advantages

Segments aligned with business goals, personalized strategies per group, more transparent decisions, and resources targeted where potential is highest.

Challenges

Needs large labeled datasets; segments drift over time; over-segmentation hurts interpretability; unbalanced data biases results; complex models give less explainable segments.

Future trends

Explainable AI, AutoML pipelines, dynamic real-time segmentation, ethical and fair modelling, and hybrids with clustering and deep learning.

Identifying informative attributes

Finding which features actually matter for prediction

Informative attributes Variables that have a significant influence on the target outcome. They carry information that helps a model tell classes apart or predict values accurately.

Why it matters

Less noise, higher accuracy and interpretability, faster training, less overfitting, and insights that are easier to act on.

Link to feature selection

Feature selection is how informative attributes are found. It separates informative (predictive), redundant (duplicated information) and irrelevant (no predictive value) features.

An attribute is informative if it…

  • shows significant correlation or association with the target,
  • varies meaningfully across samples (not constant or random),
  • provides unique information not already captured by other features,
  • reduces uncertainty in predicting the outcome.

Benefits

Challenges

Informative features change over time (concept drift); simple correlation misses hidden interactions; statistical importance isn't the same as business importance, so domain knowledge matters.

Identifying informative attributes is the foundation of every predictive model: the right features give accurate, stable and explainable predictions.

Progressive attributive segmentation

Splitting data step by step, most informative attribute first

Progressive attributive segmentation divides data into meaningful groups in stages. Progressive means attributes are applied one stage at a time, in order of predictive power; attributive means each stage is guided by the attribute that best differentiates the data at that level. You start with the most informative attribute, split, then keep dividing each group with the next most informative attribute until the segments are meaningful and stable.

Conceptual example: churn

  1. Customer satisfaction score

    Split into high and low satisfaction.

  2. Monthly usage, within low satisfaction

    Split into low and high usage.

  3. Support tickets, within low satisfaction + low usage

    Split into frequent and few complaints.

Segments: high satisfaction → low churn risk; low satisfaction + high usage → moderate risk; low satisfaction + low usage + frequent complaints → high risk.

Process

StepDescriptionOutcome
1. Attribute rankingRank variables by informativeness (correlation, information gain…)Ordered attribute list
2. Initial segmentationSplit on the most important attributePrimary segments
3. Progressive refinementWithin each segment, apply the next most informative attributeSub-segments
4. Stopping criteriaStop when segments are too small or splits add no valueFinal segments
5. InterpretationLabel and describe the segmentsActionable insights

Relationship to decision trees: each split works like a tree split, with the highest-information attributes at the top. But progressive segmentation can be guided manually by business logic as well as statistics, making it more flexible and interpretable.

Key principles

Progression: attributes in descending importance. Purity increase: each split makes segments more homogeneous. Interpretability: every split is explainable. Parsimony: fewer, meaningful groups beat many tiny ones.

Choosing attributes

Information gain or entropy (classification), Gini index (tree analysis), ANOVA / F-test (continuous targets), chi-square (categorical targets), mutual information (mixed types).

Benefits

Hierarchical, interpretable structure; clear explanations and data storytelling; strategic use in marketing and risk; avoids black-box behavior.

Limitations

Depends on the right attribute order; manual design can be subjective; may miss non-linear interactions; sensitive to outliers and correlated features; needs revalidation as data changes.

Evaluating segments: purity (members share the same outcome), stability (consistent on new data), size balance (each large enough to matter), predictive accuracy, and business interpretability. Common visuals are decision tree diagrams, heatmaps, profile tables, and cluster-and-attribute maps. Future trends include hybrid and dynamic segmentation, SHAP or LIME explanations of each step, and automated attribute ranking with AutoML.

Model induction and prediction

How a model learns, and how it is used

Model induction and prediction are the two core stages of predictive analytics. Induction answers "how does the model learn from past data?" and prediction answers "how does it use what it learned to forecast new outcomes?"

Y = f(X) + ε  →  induction estimates f* from data  →  prediction: Ŷ = f*(X′)

X are the inputs, Y the outcome, f the true relationship and ε random error. Induction produces an estimate f*, for a linear model its parameters β₀, β₁, …, βₙ. Prediction applies f* to new inputs X′.

ComparisonModel inductionPrediction
PurposeLearn from historical dataApply knowledge to new data
PhaseTrainingApplication
Data usedTraining setTest or real-world data
OutcomeParameters, rules or weightsPredicted values or categories
GoalMinimize errorGeneralize accurately

Steps in model induction

  1. Data collection

    Gather enough relevant data.

  2. Preprocessing

    Clean, normalize and transform.

  3. Feature selection

    Identify informative attributes.

  4. Algorithm selection

    Regression, tree, and so on.

  5. Training

    Fit the model's parameters on training data.

  6. Validation

    Measure performance on unseen data.

  7. Optimization

    Tune hyperparameters and regularization.

Induction approaches: empirical (learn patterns directly from data, like regression and trees), symbolic (derive rules and logic), statistical (probabilistic inference, like Bayesian models) and computational (algorithms that optimize learning, like neural networks and SVMs).

Overfitting, generalization and the model lifecycle

Induction must balance underfitting (too simple, misses patterns) and overfitting (too complex, memorizes noise). The goal is generalization: good performance on unseen data, helped by cross-validation, regularization, pruning and dropout. (Session 12 has an interactive overfitting demo.) A model's lifecycle runs through data preparation, induction, validation, prediction, monitoring, and updating as the data changes.

Challenges

Data quality (missing, biased, unbalanced), model drift, the interpretability vs. complexity trade-off, fairness and privacy, and the need for continuous validation.

Future directions

AutoML, explainable predictions with SHAP and LIME, adaptive learning as new data arrives, hybrid statistical and AI induction, and ethical AI.

Session 13 in summary

  • Predictive modelling moves from correlation, to prediction, to supervised segmentation: groups defined by predicted outcomes.
  • Supervised segmentation is guided by a known target, unlike clustering; evaluate it with lift, gains, KS and stability.
  • Informative attributes are significant, variable, unique and uncertainty-reducing; drop irrelevant, redundant, sparse and constant ones.
  • Progressive attributive segmentation splits on the most informative attribute first, then refines each segment, stopping before segments become too small.
  • Induction learns f* from training data; prediction applies it to new data. The goal is generalization, not memorization.
