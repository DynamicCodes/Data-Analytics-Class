Sampling

Studying a part to learn about the whole

We often want to know the characteristics of a large group, the population, but studying every individual is usually impossible. Instead we collect data from a smaller group, a sample, and use it to estimate the population's characteristics. That process involves sampling and estimation.

Population

The entire set of individuals, items or data points of interest. Example: all students in a university.

Sample

A smaller subset selected for analysis. Example: 100 randomly chosen students from that university.

Sampling The process of selecting a subset from a population to gather information. It saves time, money and effort while still giving meaningful insight. Good sampling gives every individual a known and fair chance of being selected, so the conclusions can be trusted for the whole population.

Estimation

Using a sample to estimate what we can't measure directly

After collecting a sample, we use it to estimate unknown population parameters, such as a mean or a proportion. The number calculated from the sample is called a statistic or estimator.

Parameter (population, unknown)Estimator (sample, calculated)
Mean μSample mean x̄
Proportion pSample proportion p̂

Types of estimation

Point estimation

A single best guess of a parameter. The sample mean x̄ is a point estimate of μ.

Interval estimation

A range within which the parameter is expected to lie. A 95% confidence interval for μ might be (45.5, 54.5).

Properties of a good estimator

Unbiasedness

Its expected value equals the true parameter: on average it hits the target.

Consistency

It becomes more accurate as the sample size grows.

Efficiency

It has the smallest variance among unbiased estimators: the tightest spread.

Sufficiency

It uses all the relevant information in the data.

Confidence intervals

A range, and how much to trust it

A confidence interval gives a range around the sample statistic that is likely to contain the population parameter. For a population mean with σ known:

CI = x̄ ± Zα/2 × σ ÷ √n

Here x̄ is the sample mean, σ the population standard deviation, n the sample size, and Zα/2 the critical value from the standard normal table: 1.645 for 90%, 1.96 for 95%, 2.576 for 99% confidence. The part after ± is the margin of error.

What "95% confident" means

✗ Wrong

There's a 95% chance μ is in this interval.

Once the interval is calculated, μ either is or isn't in it. The interval varies from sample to sample; the truth doesn't.

✗ Also wrong

95% of employees earn within this range.

That would be a range for individuals. A confidence interval is about the mean, not individual values.

✓ Correct

This method captures μ 95% of the time.

If we sampled 100 times and built an interval each time, about 95 would contain μ. The confidence is in the procedure.

Why sampling and estimation matter

  • They let researchers study large populations efficiently, reducing cost and time without losing accuracy.
  • They support prediction, planning and decision making.
  • They are the foundation of hypothesis testing and statistical inference.

H₀ vs H₁, p-valueErrors and power

Estimation determines the value of a population parameter from sample data: a point estimate such as x̄ = ΣXᵢ ÷ n for μ, or an interval such as x̄ ± Zα/2 σ ÷ √n. Hypothesis testing makes decisions about population characteristics based on sample evidence.

Hypothesis testing

Does the sample support or contradict a claim?

Hypothesis testing is a formal procedure for deciding whether sample data supports or contradicts a stated assumption (a hypothesis) about the population.

  1. State the hypotheses

    The null hypothesis H₀ says there is no effect or difference; the alternative H₁ says there is. For example, H₀: μ = μ₀ vs. H₁: μ ≠ μ₀.

  2. Choose a significance level α

    Commonly 0.05 or 0.01: the probability of rejecting H₀ when it is actually true.

  3. Select the test statistic

    Z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ/√n) when σ is known; t = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (s/√n) when it isn't.

  4. Compute it from the sample data

    And find its p-value: the probability of a result at least this extreme if H₀ were true.

  5. Make a decision

    If the p-value ≤ α, reject H₀. If the p-value > α, fail to reject H₀.

Worked example: rod lengths

A machine should produce rods averaging 50 cm. A random sample of 25 rods has x̄ = 49.5 cm, and σ = 1.2 cm. At the 5% level, does the mean length differ from 50 cm?

H₀: μ = 50   H₁: μ ≠ 50
Z = (49.5 − 50) ÷ (1.2/√25) = −0.5 ÷ 0.24 ≈ −2.08

The two-tailed critical value at 5% is ±1.96. Since |Z| = 2.08 > 1.96, we reject H₀: the mean rod length differs significantly from 50 cm. (Equivalently, p ≈ 0.037 < 0.05.)

Type I error

Rejecting a true null hypothesis. Its probability is α.

Type II error

Failing to reject a false null hypothesis. Its probability is β.

Power (1 − β)

The ability to correctly reject a false H₀, detecting an effect that really exists.

Confidence and significance

Estimation and testing both use a confidence level (1 − α) or significance level α. A 95% confidence level means α = 0.05, a 5% chance of error. A 99% confidence interval is wider than a 95% one, but more reliable.

Why statistical inference matters

  • It lets us generalize from a sample to the whole population.
  • It provides quantitative evidence for decision making.
  • It is essential in research, business analytics, healthcare studies and quality control.

Session 8 in summary

  • Sampling collects representative data; probability methods give every member a known chance and avoid the bias of convenience or judgment samples.
  • Estimation infers parameters from statistics: a point estimate is one best guess, an interval estimate is a range with a stated confidence.
  • A good estimator is unbiased, consistent, efficient and sufficient.
  • CI = x̄ ± Zα/2 σ/√n; a larger sample narrows it and a higher confidence widens it.
  • Hypothesis tests compare a test statistic with its distribution under H₀; reject H₀ when the p-value ≤ α.
  • Every test risks Type I (α) and Type II (β) errors; larger samples raise power.
