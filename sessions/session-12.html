What predictive modelling is

Using past patterns to anticipate the future

Predictive modelling The use of statistical techniques, machine learning algorithms and data mining methods to forecast future outcomes from historical data. The core idea: use past patterns to make informed predictions about future events.

Examples

Predicting customer churn, forecasting stock market trends, estimating next quarter's sales, predicting disease risk.

Key components

Data: past records or observations. Algorithms: statistical or machine learning models. Output: probabilities, forecasts or classifications.

The mathematical idea

Y = f(X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ) + ε

Y is the outcome we want to predict, X₁ … Xₙ are the predictors (features), f is the relationship the model learns from data, and ε is random error that no model can explain. A good model captures f without also memorizing ε.

Time series in practice: forecasting demand

Time series models use the order of observations. Exponential smoothing forecasts the next value as a weighted average of the past, giving recent months more weight. The smoothing factor α controls how quickly it reacts: close to 1, it follows the latest value; close to 0, it averages over a long history.

Forecastt+1 = α × Actualt + (1 − α) × Forecastt

Ensembles: why many models beat one

Majority vote of many models

Interactive

Each model is right with some probability. If the models make independent mistakes, a majority vote is right far more often than any single model. Real models share mistakes, which limits the gain.

The modelling workflow

Eight steps from question to production

  1. Problem definition

    Identify the goal: what do we want to predict?

  2. Data collection

    Gather historical, structured and unstructured data.

  3. Data cleaning and preprocessing

    Handle missing values and outliers; normalize and encode.

  4. Feature engineering

    Select important predictors using correlation, information gain or feature importance.

  5. Model selection

    Choose an algorithm suited to the problem type.

  6. Model training

    Fit the model to training data and tune hyperparameters.

  7. Model evaluation

    Use metrics such as accuracy, R², RMSE, precision, recall and AUC-ROC.

  8. Deployment and monitoring

    Integrate the model into real systems and monitor for drift or declining accuracy.

Benefits and challenges

What predictive modelling offers, and what gets in the way

Strategic benefits

Data-driven decisions, more accurate forecasts, and better customer personalization.

Operational benefits

Automates complex tasks such as risk scoring, cuts costs by anticipating failures or churn, and improves logistics and resource allocation.

Analytical benefits

Reveals hidden patterns and trends, and supports real-time decisions.

ChallengeDescription
Data qualityMissing, inconsistent or biased data distorts results
OverfittingPerforms well on training data but poorly on unseen data
InterpretabilityComplex models such as deep networks are hard to explain
Dynamic environmentsAccuracy declines as conditions change
Computational costLarge datasets and complex models need a lot of processing power
Ethics and privacyPredictive use of personal data can violate privacy norms

Limitations and the future

Where predictive modelling falls short, and where it is heading

Depends on history

Can't anticipate sudden, unprecedented events, such as COVID-19.

Assumption bias

Models inherit the patterns, and the biases, in their training data.

Over- and underfitting

Models that are too complex or too simple distort predictions.

Black boxes

Neural networks and other complex models lack transparency.

Constant change

Models need regular updates as new data arrives.

Ethical risks

Can lead to discrimination or unfair predictions, as with biased hiring models.

The future of predictive modelling

  • Automation and AutoML: automated model selection, tuning and deployment (Google AutoML, H2O.ai).
  • AI integration: combining prediction with language processing, computer vision and generative AI.
  • Real-time systems: predictions at the edge, in IoT devices and autonomous vehicles.
  • Explainable AI: tools like SHAP and LIME that explain complex models' decisions.
  • Responsible AI: a focus on bias detection, fairness and transparency.
  • Predictive + prescriptive: moving from "what will happen?" to "what should we do next?"

Session 12 in summary

  • Predictive modelling learns Y = f(X) + ε from historical data to forecast outcomes.
  • Model families match the output: regression for numbers, classification for labels, time series for sequences, clustering for groups, plus ensembles and deep learning.
  • The workflow runs from problem definition through data preparation, feature engineering, training and evaluation, to deployment and monitoring.
  • The central risk is overfitting: judge models on unseen data, not training data.
  • Data quality, interpretability, changing conditions and ethics limit what models can do; AutoML, explainable AI and responsible AI are shaping what comes next.
