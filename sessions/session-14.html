Supervised segmentation

Segments guided by a known outcome

Supervised segmentation divides data into distinct, meaningful groups based on a known target variable. It is called supervised because known outcomes guide the segmentation, unlike clustering, which relies only on similarities in the data. In essence, it identifies how the predictors explain differences in the target.

Purpose

Understand which features influence the target, form homogeneous subgroups that behave similarly, and enable targeted decisions such as risk-based strategies or personalized interventions.

Key concept

It combines predictive modelling (to estimate outcomes) with segmentation (to divide the population by them): a predictive model with interpretable group boundaries.

Theoretical foundation

Given predictors X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ and a target Y, supervised segmentation finds segments S₁, S₂, …, Sk of the predictor space such that the expected outcome differs between them:

E[Y | X ∈ Si] is distinct from E[Y | X ∈ Sj] for every i ≠ j

Each segment is defined by conditions on X, and within a segment the outcome should be as uniform as possible.

Conceptual example: churn

The most informative attribute is subscription length: long subscriptions (over 12 months) churn little, short ones (under 6 months) a lot. Within the short-term group, complaint frequency splits further. The segments:

Segment A

Long subscription → low churn.

Segment B

Short subscription + few complaints → medium churn.

Segment C

Short subscription + frequent complaints → high churn.

As in the slides, customers with 6–12 month subscriptions don't fall into any of these three segments. A complete segmentation must cover everyone, for example by making the first split "12 months or less" vs. "more than 12 months".

Effective segmentation

Segments differ significantly in outcomes, are interpretable and actionable, are large enough to be reliable, stay stable over time, and improve decisions.

Advantages

Data-driven and target-specific; supports precision marketing and personalization; interpretable through rules; focuses on relevant patterns; bridges descriptive and predictive analytics.

Challenges

Needs labeled data; segments drift over time; too many attributes risk overfitting; can be expensive on large data; may ignore relationships not linked to the target.

Case study: a telecom company predicting churn from tenure, usage, service calls and billing found three segments: long tenure with few service calls (loyal), new users with many calls (high churn risk), and medium tenure with moderate calls (at risk, ideal for retention offers). Future trends include explainable segmentation, real-time dynamic segments, hybrid models, AutoML-chosen thresholds, and fairness checks.

Visualizing segmentations

Making segments interpretable, communicable and actionable

Once data is segmented, visualization turns numerical results into intuitive patterns that show how segments differ in behavior, composition and outcomes. It helps you understand the differences, evaluate whether segments are well separated, communicate with non-technical audiences, and guide actions such as targeted marketing or risk management.

Focus on the target: how segments differ in predicted outcomes, where the decision boundaries fall, and whether predictions are consistent. Typical visuals: tree diagrams, partial dependence plots, confusion matrices by segment, target rate per segment.

Trees as a set of rules

Every path from root to leaf is an if–then rule

Decision trees are among the most intuitive and interpretable predictive models. The root node represents the whole dataset, branches are decisions on feature values, and leaves are the final predictions. Each split is a rule learned from the data that partitions it into more homogeneous subsets, so the whole tree is a set of if–then rules.

From splits to rules

Algorithms such as CART, ID3 and C4.5 choose each split with a measure like information gain (entropy reduction), the Gini index or the chi-square statistic. If "Age ≤ 30" produces the most homogeneous subsets, it becomes the root split, and the first condition of every rule.

  1. Train a decision tree

    On labeled data.

  2. Trace each path

    From the root node to every leaf.

  3. Combine the conditions

    Join the split conditions along the path with AND.

  4. Assign the conclusion

    The leaf's class or value becomes the rule's THEN part.

Advantages

Easy to visualize and interpret; handles numerical and categorical data; needs little preprocessing; converts directly into rule-based decision systems; great for segmenting customers, risks or behaviors.

Limitations

Large trees produce too many, complex rules; overfitting without pruning; small data changes can produce different rules (instability).

Probability estimation

Trees give probabilities, not just labels

Every leaf of a decision tree holds a subset of the training data that share similar characteristics. Each leaf stores a predicted class (the majority class) and a probability estimate: the fraction of its samples in each class. That makes trees probabilistic models, not just rule-based classifiers.

P(c | L) = number of training samples of class c in leaf L ÷ total samples in L

For example, a leaf with 8 "Buy = Yes" and 2 "Buy = No" customers predicts Yes with P = 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8. The same calculation applies at every node during training (P(c | N) = nc ÷ n), and the probabilities in a node always sum to 1.

Why it mattersExplanation
UncertaintyGives confidence in a prediction instead of a bare yes/no
Threshold-based decisionsFor example, approve a loan only if P(default) < 0.3
RankingSort cases by likelihood, useful in marketing and fraud detection
CalibrationCombines well with ensembles such as random forests and gradient boosting

In ensembles

P(c | X) = (1/T) Σt Pt(c | X)

A random forest averages the leaf probabilities from its T trees, which gives smoother, more reliable estimates than one tree.

Laplace smoothing

P(c | L) = (nc + 1) ÷ (n + k)

nc is the class count in the leaf, n the leaf size and k the number of classes. It stops small leaves from claiming certainty: a leaf with 3 of 3 "Yes" becomes 4/5 = 0.8 instead of 1.0.
