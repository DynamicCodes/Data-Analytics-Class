Random variables

Turning the outcomes of an experiment into numbers

Random variable A variable whose numerical value depends on the outcome of a random experiment. Formally, it is a function that assigns a real number to each outcome in a sample space S: X : S → ℝ, so each outcome s ∈ S gets the number X(s).

Random variables connect random events to numbers, which lets us apply mathematics to probability.

Example 1: coin toss

S = {H, T}. Define X(H) = 1 and X(T) = 0, so X is 1 for a head and 0 for a tail.

Example 2: rolling a die

S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Define X(s) = s, so X takes the values 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6.

Probability distributions

How likely each value of a random variable is

Every random variable has a probability distribution describing how its values are spread. How it is written depends on the type of variable.

Discrete: probability mass function (PMF)

P(X = xi)

Gives the probability of each exact value. The probabilities must add up to 1: Σ P(X = xi) = 1. For a fair die, P(X = x) = 1/6 for x = 1 to 6.

Continuous: probability density function (PDF)

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx

Probabilities are areas under the curve f(x), and the total area is 1. The probability of any single exact value is 0, so only intervals make sense.

Expected value (mean)

The long-run average value of a random variable.

Discrete: E[X] = Σ xi P(X = xi)
Continuous: E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx

Variance and standard deviation

How spread out the values are around the mean.

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²]    σ = √Var(X)

A common example of a continuous distribution is the normal distribution, whose density is f(x) = (1 ÷ σ√(2π)) · e−(x − μ)² ÷ 2σ², where μ is the mean and σ the standard deviation.

Distributions and data

The bridge between theory and what we observe

A probability distribution describes how likely each possible value of a random variable is. It is the bridge between theoretical probability and real-world data: data collected from experiments or observations can be used to estimate a distribution.

  1. Collect data

    From experiments, surveys, sensors and so on.

  2. Organize data

    Into frequency tables or histograms.

  3. Approximate probabilities

    Relative frequency = count of an outcome ÷ total outcomes.

  4. Fit a theoretical distribution

    Such as binomial, Poisson or normal.

Example: toss a coin 10 times and get 6 heads and 4 tails. The estimated probabilities (0.6, 0.4) form an empirical distribution, which you can compare with the theoretical distribution (0.5, 0.5).

Binomial, Poisson and normal

The three distributions you will meet most often

Binomial (discrete)

The number of successes in n independent trials, each with two outcomes (success or failure) and success probability p.

P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n − k

Mean np, variance np(1 − p). Symmetric when p = 0.5, skewed otherwise.

Example: toss a fair coin 3 times. P(X = 2) = C(3, 2)(0.5)²(0.5) = 3 × 0.125 = 0.375.

Poisson (discrete)

The number of events in a fixed interval of time or space, when events occur independently at an average rate λ.

P(X = k) = e−λ λk ÷ k!

Mean and variance are both λ.

Example: a clinic averages 4 patients an hour. P(X = 2) = e−4 × 4² ÷ 2! = 8e−4 ≈ 0.146.

Normal (continuous)

Continuous data that clusters around a mean: the bell curve.

f(x) = e−(x − μ)² ÷ 2σ² ÷ σ√(2π)

Symmetric and bell-shaped; mean = median = mode; total area 1. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

The standard normal distribution

A normal distribution with μ = 0 and σ = 1 is the standard normal. Any normal value can be converted to it with a z-score, which counts how many standard deviations the value lies from the mean:

Z = (X − μ) ÷ σ

Heights, blood pressure, test scores and machine errors approximately follow a normal distribution, so z-scores let you compare them on one scale. The area explorer above shows z-scores for any height range.

How the three connect

From discrete counts to a continuous curve

Binomial → Poisson

For large n and small p, with np = λ, the binomial becomes a Poisson distribution.

Poisson → Normal

For large λ, the Poisson distribution looks normal, with mean and variance λ.

A progression

All three are mathematically linked, moving from discrete to continuous behavior.

The central limit theorem

Why the bell curve is everywhere

The central limit theorem (CLT) is one of the most important results in statistics. It explains how the distribution of sample means behaves when we take repeated samples from any population, whatever the population's shape.

Central limit theorem When independent random samples of size n are taken from any population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as n becomes large.

Mathematical expression

If X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ are independent random variables with mean μ and variance σ², the sample mean is X̄ = (1/n) Σ Xi, and

Z = (X̄ − μ) ÷ (σ / √n) → N(0, 1) as n → ∞

Key idea

No matter how skewed or non-normal the population, the distribution of sample means becomes approximately normal for large n. The larger n is, the closer it gets. In most practical cases, n ≥ 30 is large enough.

Parameters of the sampling distribution

μX̄ = μ
σX̄ = σ / √n

The spread of sample means, σ/√n, is called the standard error.

A population has μ = 70 and σ = 10. For samples of n = 36, the sample mean has mean 70 and standard error 10 ÷ √36 = 10 ÷ 6 ≈ 1.67.

Why the CLT matters

Enables inference

Lets us use normal models for hypothesis tests and confidence intervals.

Simplifies problems

Works even when the population is not normal.

A foundation

Underpins regression, ANOVA and control charts.

Real-world applications: estimating population parameters from samples, quality control and process monitoring, election polling and surveys, and financial modeling and risk estimation.

Session 7 in summary

  • A random variable maps each outcome to a number; it can be discrete (counts) or continuous (measurements).
  • Discrete distributions use a PMF where probabilities add to 1; continuous ones use a PDF where probability is area under the curve.
  • Data estimates a distribution through relative frequencies, which settle on the true probabilities as data grows.
  • Binomial counts successes in n trials, Poisson counts events in an interval, and the normal describes continuous data around a mean. They are linked as limits of one another.
  • The CLT says sample means are approximately normal for large samples, with mean μ and standard error σ/√n, whatever the population's shape. That is why the normal distribution is the backbone of statistical inference.
