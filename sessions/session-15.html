What prescriptive modelling is

From "what will happen?" to "what should we do?"

Prescriptive modelling The use of mathematical, statistical and computational techniques to recommend decisions or actions based on predictive insights and constraints. It combines predictive models (what is likely to happen), optimization models (what is the best action) and simulation models (what-if scenarios) to guide decisions under uncertainty.

How it works: three stages

  1. Input: data and predictions

    Historical data, outputs of predictive models (probabilities, forecasts), and business constraints (resources, budgets, time limits).

  2. Decision / optimization

    Mathematical models such as linear or integer programming, with an objective (maximize profit, minimize risk) and constraints (operational or resource limits).

  3. Output: recommendations

    Actionable strategies, such as pricing plans or resource allocations, ranked by expected impact.

The mathematical formulation

Maximize or minimize Z = f(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ)   subject to   gᵢ(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ) ≤ bᵢ,   i = 1, 2, …, m

Z is the objective (profit, cost, risk), the xᵢ are the decision variables we control (how much to produce, where to send a truck), and each gᵢ ≤ bᵢ is a constraint with limit bᵢ (machine hours, budget, drivers). The classic example is a product-mix problem:

Benefits

Actionable insights that move from prediction to decision; optimization under constraints; dynamic decisions that adapt to real-time feedback; better ROI; the backbone of automated AI decision systems.

Challenges

Complex integration of several methods; bad predictions lead to bad prescriptions; large optimizations are computationally heavy; recommendations must be explainable; real-time systems need continuous updates.

The future

Integration with AI and reinforcement learning, adaptive systems that keep learning, real-time prescriptions inside operational systems, and explainable AI for trust.

From a churn prediction to a retention decision

The predictive model says "this customer has an 80% chance of churning". The prescriptive model decides what to do, for example "offer a 20% discount to customers with more than 70% churn probability". But is 70% the right cut-off? That depends on the costs and benefits, which is exactly what prescriptive analytics works out.

How prescriptive analytics works

Predictive insights + optimization + constraints and objectives

Data → Prediction → Optimization → Action

  1. Data collection and preparation

    Transactions, sensors and IoT, historical performance, and external data (market, weather, social media), cleaned and integrated. A logistics company gathers past delivery times, routes, fuel use and traffic.

  2. Predictive modelling

    Forecast demand, risks, customer behavior, costs or delays, for example the probability that each delivery route will be delayed.

  3. Define objectives and constraints

    The objective to maximize or minimize (profit, cost, speed) and the real-world limits (trucks, budgets, working hours, service levels).

  4. Optimization and simulation

    The core engine: linear/integer programming, dynamic programming, genetic algorithms, Monte Carlo simulation and reinforcement learning test what-if scenarios to find the best actions under uncertainty.

  5. Recommendation and implementation

    Which action to take, when, with what resources, delivered to managers or automated, for example "assign driver A to route 5 and driver B to route 7 to cut cost by 12%".

Simulation: deciding under uncertainty

Forecasts are never exact. Monte Carlo simulation runs a decision against thousands of possible futures to find the choice with the best expected result. The classic case is stocking for an uncertain demand: order too little and you lose sales; order too much and you're left with unsold stock.

Session 15 in summary

  • Prescriptive modelling recommends actions by combining predictions, optimization and simulation under real constraints.
  • It is the last step of the continuum: descriptive (what happened), diagnostic (why), predictive (what next), prescriptive (what to do).
  • An optimization model has an objective, decision variables and constraints; for linear programs the best solution lies at a corner of the feasible region.
  • Prescriptive decisions depend on costs and benefits as well as predictions, so the best action threshold changes when the economics change.
  • Simulation handles uncertainty by testing decisions against many possible futures.
  • Prescriptive analytics closes the loop between prediction and action.
