Chi-Square Test, Information Gain and Skewness

Three tools for understanding categorical data and distributions: the chi-square test for whether counts match expectations, entropy-based measures of how much a feature tells you about a target, and skewness for how lopsided a distribution is.

The chi-square test

Do observed counts match what we'd expect?

Chi-square (χ²) test A non-parametric hypothesis test, developed by Karl Pearson in 1900, that checks whether there is a significant association between categorical variables, or whether observed frequencies differ from expected frequencies. It doesn't assume a normal distribution, and it is based on the chi-square distribution: the distribution of a sum of squared standard normal variables.
TypePurposeExample
Goodness of fitDoes an observed frequency distribution match an expected one?Do dice rolls follow a uniform distribution?
Test of independenceAre two categorical variables related?Is gender related to product preference?

The formula

χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² ÷ Ei

Oi is each observed frequency and Ei the frequency expected if H₀ were true. Each term measures how far one cell is from expectation, scaled by its size; the terms are squared, so χ² is never negative.

Computation steps

  1. State the hypotheses

    Goodness of fit: H₀, the observed frequencies fit the expected distribution; H₁, they don't. Independence: H₀, the variables are independent; H₁, they are associated.

  2. Find expected frequencies

    From the hypothesized distribution, or for a two-way table: Eij = (row totali × column totalj) ÷ grand total.

  3. Compute χ²

    Add up (O − E)² ÷ E over every category or cell.

  4. Find the degrees of freedom

    Goodness of fit: df = k − 1 for k categories. Independence: df = (r − 1)(c − 1) for r rows and c columns.

  5. Decide

    If χ²calculated > χ²table (or p ≤ α), reject H₀. Otherwise, fail to reject H₀.

Assumptions

  • Data are counts (frequencies), not percentages.
  • Observations are independent.
  • Categories are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
  • Each expected frequency is generally at least 5.
  • The sample is randomly selected.

Test of independence

Are two categorical variables related?

Applications, advantages and limitations

Applications

Independence of attributes (education level and employment type), goodness of fit of models, market research, genetics, psychology and social sciences, and checking the randomness of categorical data.

Advantages

Simple and widely applicable, works with nominal (categorical) data, and doesn't require a normal distribution.

Limitations

Sensitive to sample size: large samples can make tiny differences significant. Unreliable when expected frequencies are below 5, and can't be applied directly to continuous data.

The chi-square distribution

What χ² looks like when H₀ is true

  • It is positively skewed (asymmetric).
  • Its shape depends on the degrees of freedom.
  • As df increases, it approaches a normal distribution.
  • χ² values are never negative, since they are sums of squares.

A low χ² means observed and expected frequencies agree (fail to reject H₀). A high χ² means a poor fit or a significant association (reject H₀).

Information gain and mutual information

Measuring how much a feature tells you about the target

To build effective models, we need to know how relevant each feature is to the target. Information gain (IG) and mutual information (MI) quantify that relevance and dependency, and are used in feature selection, dimensionality reduction and decision trees. Both are built on entropy, a measure of uncertainty.

Entropy

H(D) = −Σ P(xi) log₂ P(xi)

0 when the outcome is certain; 1 bit for a 50/50 yes/no split.

Conditional entropy

H(D | A) = Σ P(aj) H(D | aj)

The uncertainty left once feature A is known: a weighted average over A's values.

Information gain

IG(D, A) = H(D) − H(D | A)

How much knowing A reduces uncertainty about the target. Decision trees split on the feature with the highest IG, giving more homogeneous nodes.

Mutual information

Mutual information measures the mutual dependence between two random variables: how much information you get about one by observing the other. It is never negative, and higher MI means stronger dependence. For a feature and a target it is calculated exactly like information gain, and it is symmetric: MI(X; Y) = MI(Y; X).

Advantages of IG

Simple to compute; effective for choosing splits in decision trees, which improves accuracy and reduces overfitting; intuitive to interpret.

Limitations of IG

Looks at one feature at a time, so it misses interactions; biased toward features with many categories (try "Day number" above). The gain ratio corrects this bias.

Advantages of MI

Captures linear and non-linear relationships; used in feature selection, clustering and dimensionality reduction; works with discrete and continuous variables.

Limitations of MI

Estimates for continuous data depend on how values are binned or scaled; affected by noise; computationally heavy for large, high-dimensional data.

For a single feature and a target, IG and MI give the same number, as the worked example shows. Both measure one pair of variables at a time; capturing interactions between several features needs multivariate methods.

Skewness

How lopsided is the distribution?

Skewness A measure of asymmetry in the distribution of a variable: how much, and in which direction, it deviates from perfect symmetry. In a symmetric distribution the mean, median and mode are equal; in a skewed one they differ.

Population skewness

Skewness = E[(X − μ)³] ÷ σ³

X is the variable, μ its mean, σ its standard deviation and E the expected value.

Sample skewness

g₁ = n ÷ [(n − 1)(n − 2)] × Σ((Xi − X̄) ÷ s)³

n is the sample size, X̄ the sample mean and s the sample standard deviation.

TypeShapeMean, median and mode
Zero skewnessSymmetricMean = median = mode
Positive (right) skewMost values on the left, long tail on the rightMean > median > mode
Negative (left) skewMost values on the right, long tail on the leftMean < median < mode

Why skewness matters

Direction

Shows which way data deviates from normality.

Model choice

Helps choose an appropriate statistical model.

Finance

Returns are often skewed, which matters for risk analysis.

Transformation

Guides log or square-root transforms before tests that assume normality.

For a normal distribution, skewness is 0; the further from 0, the more asymmetric the distribution. Skewness also makes the mean and standard deviation less reliable summaries, so correcting it matters before parametric tests like t-tests and ANOVA.

Session 11 in summary

  • The chi-square test compares observed and expected counts: goodness of fit for one variable, independence for two.
  • χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E; compare it with the table value for the right degrees of freedom, and check that expected counts are at least 5.
  • Entropy measures uncertainty; information gain is how much a feature reduces it, and decision trees split on the highest gain.
  • Mutual information measures shared information between two variables; for a feature and a target it equals information gain.
  • Skewness measures asymmetry: positive skew has a long right tail (mean > median); transformations like log or square root can reduce it.
