What is business analytics

Understanding the foundations

Business analytics The practice of using statistical analysis and data mining to drive business decisions and strategies. It covers a range of techniques that help organizations understand their data and use it for competitive advantage.

In today's data-driven environment, analytics is what keeps organizations competitive. It lets companies make informed decisions, optimize their operations and improve customer experiences, all based on insight drawn from data rather than instinct alone.

This session moves in two parts. First we look at the kinds of analytics a business can do and how three industries use them. Then we follow a data analytics project through its life cycle, one phase at a time.

Three types of analytics

Each type answers a different business question

Descriptive

What happened? Summarizes historical data to find trends and patterns. Most reports and dashboards are descriptive.

Predictive

What could happen? Uses statistical models and machine learning to forecast future outcomes from historical data.

Prescriptive

What should we do? Combines descriptive and predictive insight to recommend the best action.

Industry case studies

How analytics changes the way three industries work

Case study 1: Retail analytics

The industry
Retail moves fast, with constant shifts in consumer behavior and market conditions. Analytics helps retailers adapt to those shifts and run their operations more efficiently.
Data sources
Point-of-sale systems, customer transaction records, social media interactions and inventory management systems.
Findings and impact
Retailers have spotted trends in consumer preferences, tuned inventory levels and sharpened marketing, leading to clear gains in sales and customer satisfaction.

Case study 2: Financial services

The industry
Financial services depend heavily on analytics to manage risk, optimize portfolios and improve customer experience. Analytics is built into everyday decision making.
Techniques applied
Risk modeling, credit scoring and fraud detection help firms assess potential risks and make informed investment decisions.
Results for risk management
Firms identify threats earlier and reduce them more effectively, protecting assets and improving profitability.

Case study 3: Healthcare analytics

The challenges
Healthcare data is fragmented, privacy-sensitive and complex, which calls for robust analytics to draw meaningful insight from it.
Methods used
Predictive modeling, patient segmentation and outcome analysis to improve care delivery and operational efficiency.
Outcomes
Better patient outcomes, lower costs and more efficient operations, which together raise the quality of care.

Tools and challenges

Navigating the analytics landscape

Choosing analytics tools

Business analytics tools range from simple visualization software to complex machine learning platforms. When comparing them, look at ease of use, how well they integrate with your systems, how far they scale and how much advanced analysis they support.

The right tool depends on your business needs, your data infrastructure and the skills of the people who will use it. A tool only helps if it fits all three.

  • Excel
  • Tableau
  • Power BI
  • Python (pandas, Matplotlib)
  • R
  • SAS

Challenges in implementing analytics

Data quality issues

Poor data quality undermines analytics. Data cleansing and validation need to be a priority.

Resistance to change

Cultural resistance slows adoption. Build a data-driven culture and show people the benefits.

Skill gaps

A shortage of skilled analysts is a real barrier. Invest in training to build capability in-house.

Key takeaways and future trends

The case studies show analytics improving performance and decisions across very different industries. Its future will be shaped by artificial intelligence, machine learning and big data, bringing more sophisticated analysis within reach.

Organizations that treat analytics as a core part of strategy, and invest in the tools, training and culture to support it, get the most from their data.

The data analytics life cycle

Understanding the framework

Data analytics The systematic computational analysis of data sets to uncover patterns, correlations and insights that inform decision making.

Analytics gives decision makers evidence to support strategic choices, improve operational efficiency and drive innovation. The life cycle organizes that work into stages. Each stage feeds the next, and the last one loops back to the first, because every result raises new questions.

Discovery

Exploring data to find what is worth analyzing

Discovery data analytics Analyzing data to uncover patterns, trends and insights that inform decisions. It emphasizes exploration and generating hypotheses, not only confirming theories you already hold.

Key concepts

Data sources and types

Structured databases, unstructured social media data and sensor data from IoT devices.

Collection methods

Surveys, web scraping and API integrations, each with its own strengths and weaknesses.

Data context

When, where and under what circumstances the data was collected all shape how it should be read.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

EDA is how analysts get to know a dataset. Summary statistics, correlation analysis and clustering reveal its structure and the relationships between variables. Charts make those patterns visible, and help you spot trends over time and anomalies that may point to real events or to errors in collection.

Tools for discovery

Tableau, Power BI and Python libraries such as pandas and Matplotlib are widely used. Compare them on visualization capability, ease of use, integration and scalability, and choose based on the complexity of your data, the skills of your users and what the analysis needs to deliver.

Interpreting and acting on insights

The value of discovery lies in turning raw data into insight someone can act on. Organizations then build those insights into strategy, measure success with clear metrics and KPIs, and refine their approach through a continuous feedback loop.

Watch for common pitfalls: bias, and reading more into a pattern than the data supports. Both lead to flawed conclusions and poor decisions.

Data preparation

The foundation of every analysis

Data preparation The process of cleaning, transforming and organizing raw data into a usable format for analysis.

Data preparation involves collecting, cleaning, transforming and structuring data so it can be used for analysis, visualization and modeling. Well-prepared data leads to more accurate insights and more reliable business decisions. Poor data quality, on the other hand, leads to misleading conclusions.

Six key steps

  1. Data collection

    Gather data from databases, APIs, spreadsheets, sensors, web logs and social media. Make sure it is relevant and accessible.

  2. Data cleaning

    Handle missing or inconsistent values, remove duplicates, correct typos, filter out irrelevant records, and detect and manage outliers.

  3. Data transformation

    Convert data into a consistent format. Apply normalization, scaling or encoding, and derive new variables (feature engineering).

  4. Data integration

    Merge data from several sources into one unified dataset, resolving schema mismatches and keeping references between tables intact.

  5. Data reduction

    Remove redundant features or records. Use dimensionality reduction (such as PCA) or sampling to improve performance.

  6. Data validation and quality assurance

    Check accuracy, completeness and consistency using validation rules and profiling checks before analysis starts.

Model planning

Deciding how to approach the analytical problem

Model planning The stage where analysts decide which statistical, mathematical or machine learning models fit the problem. It sets the roadmap for building models that uncover patterns, make predictions or provide insight.

Objectives

  • Select the most suitable analytical technique for the problem.
  • Define the target variable and the predictors.
  • Plan how data will be split for training, validation and testing.
  • Choose evaluation metrics that match business goals.

Key steps

  1. Understand the business problem

    Clarify what needs to be predicted or optimized, and translate the business objective into a question data can answer.

  2. Select modeling techniques

    Pick a family of models that matches the question (see the table below).

  3. Define data requirements

    Identify the relevant features (independent variables) and specify the target variable and data format.

  4. Set up a validation strategy

    Split data into training, validation and test sets, and use cross-validation for reliability.

  5. Select evaluation metrics

    Decide in advance how success will be measured.

DescriptiveClustering, association rulesWhat groups or patterns exist?

Time seriesForecasting, trend analysisHow will it change over time?

Model building and implementation

From a plan to a working solution

Model building and implementation Creating, training, testing and deploying analytical models to produce actionable insights or predictions. This phase turns the planned approach into a working solution.

  1. Model development

    • Apply the chosen algorithms, such as regression, decision trees, clustering or neural networks.
    • Use training data to teach the model the relationship between inputs and outputs.
    • Try several algorithms and compare them.

  2. Testing and evaluation

    • Test performance on data the model hasn't seen (the validation and test sets).
    • Evaluate with suitable metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall or RMSE.
    • Tune hyperparameters for the best results.

  3. Model optimization

    • Use feature selection and engineering to make the model more efficient.
    • Apply cross-validation, grid search and ensemble methods.

  4. Implementation (deployment)

    • Integrate the model into production systems such as dashboards, web apps or APIs.
    • Monitor model performance and data drift.
    • Retrain with new data to keep it accurate.

Quality assurance

Making sure your insights can be trusted

Quality assurance (QA) Checking that the data, processes and outputs used for analysis are accurate, consistent, reliable and actionable.

Purpose

Integrity

Data is correct and intact.

Fewer errors

Mistakes in analysis and reporting are caught.

Confidence

Decision makers can rely on the results.

Reproducibility

Results can be produced again.

Key areas of QA

Data quality

  • Accuracy: reflects the real world correctly.
  • Completeness: no gaps in critical fields.
  • Consistency: uniform across sources.
  • Timeliness: up to date and relevant.
  • Validity: follows expected formats and rules.

Process quality

ETL (extract, transform, load) pipelines work correctly, transformations and aggregations are validated, and checks for anomalies and duplicates run automatically.

Analytical quality

Models and algorithms are verified, results are cross-checked with benchmark datasets, and dashboards are checked for accuracy and clarity.

Documentation and reproducibility

Sources, cleaning steps, assumptions and methods are recorded so the analysis can be repeated with the same results.

Best practices

  • Start QA early by validating data as soon as it enters your system.
  • Automate checks wherever possible.
  • Use standard data formats and definitions.
  • Audit dashboards and models regularly.
  • Build a culture of data quality awareness in the team.

Documentation

A structured record of the whole data life cycle

Documentation is a critical part of quality assurance and reproducibility. It keeps data, processes and analyses transparent, understandable and reusable. It is more than writing notes: it is a structured record of everything that happened to the data.

Why document

  • Gives team members and stakeholders a clear picture.
  • Makes analyses and models reproducible.
  • Helps troubleshoot errors and inconsistencies.
  • Supports regulatory compliance and audits.
  • Preserves knowledge when people join or leave the team.

What to document

AreaWhat it covers
DataSources (databases, APIs, surveys, public datasets); a data dictionary with definitions, types, units, allowed values and constraints; data lineage showing origin and transformations; quality notes on known issues and how they were handled.
ProcessETL and pipeline steps; an explanation of SQL, Python or R scripts; business logic and statistical assumptions applied along the way.
AnalysisMethodology; parameter choices such as hyperparameters and thresholds; explanation of charts, dashboards and derived metrics; key findings and their context.
VersioningA change log tracking changes to data, scripts and analyses, with versions of datasets, models and reports kept for reproducibility.

Best practices

  • Use standard templates for data dictionaries, analysis reports and dashboards.
  • Keep everything in one central place, such as Confluence, GitHub or SharePoint.
  • Update documentation whenever pipelines or models change.
  • Include examples and sample outputs.
  • Write so non-technical stakeholders can follow it too.
