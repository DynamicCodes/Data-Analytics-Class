Evidence, Probability and Probabilistic Reasoning

Evidence rarely gives certainty; it changes how confident we should be. This session shows how Bayes' rule turns evidence into updated probabilities, how to combine many signals into one belief (and when that goes wrong), and how probabilistic models reason under uncertainty.

Evidence and probability

Two foundations for reasoning under uncertainty

Evidence

What we observe or know from data, experiments or experience: data or observations that support or refute a hypothesis, prediction or belief.

Probability

A numerical measure of how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), based on the available evidence.

In short: probability quantifies uncertainty, and evidence informs and updates it. Evidence doesn't give certainty; it changes our confidence about an event or hypothesis.

Bayes' theorem

The bridge between evidence and probability

Bayesian thinking lets us update a belief when new evidence arrives. Bayes' theorem links the prior belief, the evidence and the updated belief:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) ÷ P(E)
TermNameMeaning
P(H)PriorInitial belief, before seeing the evidence
P(E | H)LikelihoodHow likely the evidence is if the hypothesis is true
P(H | E)PosteriorUpdated belief after seeing the evidence
P(E)Marginal likelihoodOverall probability of the evidence; a normalizing constant

Example: a rare disease

1% of people have a rare disease, P(D) = 0.01. A test is 99% accurate: P(Pos | D) = 0.99 and P(Pos | ¬D) = 0.01. You test positive. How likely is it that you actually have the disease?

P(Pos) = 0.99 × 0.01 + 0.01 × 0.99 = 0.0198
P(D | Pos) = 0.99 × 0.01 ÷ 0.0198 ≈ 0.5

Despite a "99% accurate" test, there's only a 50% chance of having the disease, because the disease itself is rare. Session 6 walks through this example step by step.

Common tools: Bayesian networks (graphical models linking evidence and probabilities), the naive Bayes classifier (a simple model assuming conditional independence), A/B testing (experimental evidence comparing success rates), and Monte Carlo simulation (random sampling to estimate distributions).

Challenges: misreading evidence (for example, confirmation bias), poor-quality data producing misleading probabilities, overconfidence in prior assumptions, and the difficulty of quantifying uncertainty.

Evidence provides the observed reality; probability provides the quantitative belief. "Evidence changes what we believe, and probability tells us how much we should believe it."

Combining evidence

Many signals, one belief

In decision analytics we usually have several pieces of evidence E₁, E₂, E₃, … (data, signals, test results) bearing on one hypothesis H. Explicit evidence combination updates our belief about H using all of them with Bayes' rule:

P(H | E₁, E₂, …, Eₙ) = P(E₁, E₂, …, Eₙ | H) × P(H) ÷ P(E₁, E₂, …, Eₙ)

If the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given H, the joint likelihood becomes a product:

P(E₁, E₂, …, Eₙ | H) = P(E₁ | H) × P(E₂ | H) × … × P(Eₙ | H)
so   P(H | E₁, …, Eₙ) = P(H) × ∏ P(Eᵢ | H) ÷ P(E₁, …, Eₙ)

In practice, P(E₁, …, Eₙ) is found by computing the same product for every hypothesis (here, H and not H) and dividing by the total, so the posteriors add up to 1. This is exactly how the naive Bayes classifier works.

When evidence isn't independent

Don't count the same clue twice

If the pieces of evidence are dependent, you can't simply multiply their likelihoods. You must use joint or conditional probabilities, such as:

P(E₁, E₂ | H) = P(E₁ | H) × P(E₂ | E₁, H)

Multiplying anyway counts overlapping information more than once and makes the posterior overconfident. Bayesian networks handle this better by modelling the relationships between pieces of evidence explicitly.

Combining evidence with Bayes' rule turns fragmented data into a coherent belief. Each new observation refines our understanding, as long as we account for how the observations relate to each other.

Probabilistic reasoning

Degrees of belief instead of true or false

Probabilistic reasoning A logical framework that uses probability theory to represent and reason about uncertain knowledge. Instead of absolute truths (yes/no), it works with degrees of belief. It asks: "Given what I know, how likely is this to be true?"

It lets us combine prior knowledge with observed evidence, update beliefs as new data appears, and make rational decisions under uncertainty.

Session 20 in summary

  • Evidence doesn't give certainty; it changes how confident we should be. Probability measures that confidence.
  • Bayes' theorem, P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) ÷ P(E), turns a prior into a posterior; rare hypotheses stay unlikely even after strong evidence.
  • Several pieces of evidence can be combined by multiplying their likelihoods, but only if they're conditionally independent given the hypothesis (the naive Bayes assumption).
  • Dependent evidence must be combined with joint or conditional probabilities; multiplying anyway double-counts and overstates confidence.
  • Probabilistic reasoning runs forward (cause → effect), backward (effect → cause) and abductively (best explanation); Bayesian networks and Markov models put it into practice.
