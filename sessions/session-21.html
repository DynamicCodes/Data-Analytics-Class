Factor Analysis, Directional Data and Functional Data Analysis

Three specialized kinds of analysis for data that ordinary methods handle badly: many correlated variables driven by a few hidden factors, directions and cyclic times that wrap around, and observations that are entire curves rather than single numbers.

Factor analysis

A few hidden factors behind many correlated variables

Factor analysis A statistical technique that identifies underlying relationships (factors) among a large set of observed variables. It reduces dimensionality by grouping correlated variables into a smaller set of latent (unobserved) factors.

Its objectives are to identify the latent variables that explain the pattern of correlations, to reduce redundancy among variables, and to summarize the data efficiently while keeping most of its information.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

Used when you don't know the structure or number of factors: it explores the data to discover underlying dimensions, such as personality traits in questionnaire responses.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Used when you already have a theory about the structure: it tests whether the data fits, for example whether 16 personality factors fit Cattell's theory.

The mathematical model

With p observed variables X₁, X₂, …, Xₚ, each is written as a linear combination of m common factors F₁, F₂, …, Fₘ plus an error term:

Xᵢ = aᵢ₁F₁ + aᵢ₂F₂ + … + aᵢₘFₘ + eᵢ

aᵢⱼ is the factor loading (the correlation between variable i and factor j), Fⱼ is a common factor, and eᵢ is the variable's unique factor or error.

Example: five exam subjects, two abilities

Students have marks in Math, Physics, Literature, History and Art. Factor analysis finds two factors: Analytical ability (high loadings on Math and Physics) and Verbal/creative ability (high loadings on Literature, History and Art). Instead of five variables, each student is described by two latent factors.

Rotation: making factors interpretable

The first factors extracted are mathematically valid but often hard to read: every variable loads a bit on every factor. Rotation turns the factor axes so that each variable loads strongly on one factor and weakly on the others. Varimax keeps the factors uncorrelated (orthogonal); Oblimin lets them correlate (oblique). Rotating doesn't change how much variance is explained in total, only how it's shared out.

Steps

  1. Collect data

    Interval or ratio-scaled variables; check sampling adequacy (KMO above 0.6, Bartlett's test significant).

  2. Form the correlation matrix

    Correlations among all the variables.

  3. Extract factors

    With principal component analysis, principal axis factoring or maximum likelihood.

  4. Decide how many

    Eigenvalue above 1 (Kaiser's rule), the elbow of the scree plot, or the percentage of variance explained.

  5. Rotate

    Varimax (orthogonal) or Oblimin (oblique) to improve interpretability.

  6. Interpret and name

    Look for patterns in the high-loading variables and name each factor.

  7. Validate

    Cross-validate, or run a confirmatory analysis on another dataset.

DomainExample
PsychologyUnderlying traits such as intelligence or personality
MarketingHow customers perceive product attributes
FinanceReducing many financial indicators to common risk factors
EducationLatent skills behind test scores
HealthcareGrouping symptoms into diagnostic dimensions

Advantages

Reduces complexity, reveals hidden structure, improves interpretability, and removes multicollinearity.

Limitations

Needs large samples; choosing and naming factors is subjective; sensitive to outliers and scaling; captures only linear relationships.

Directional data

Data that wraps around

Directional data analytics The analysis of data that represents directions, orientations or angles rather than ordinary linear measurements. Such data is cyclic: after 360° (or 24 hours) it wraps around to the beginning.
ContextExample
MeteorologyWind direction in degrees
NavigationShip or aircraft bearing
GeologyOrientation of rock layers or faults
Time of dayTraffic peaks, purchase times
BiologyAnimal movement or migration direction
RoboticsSensor orientation or motion angle

Why it needs different methods

0° and 360° are the same direction, yet numerically far apart. The ordinary average of 350° and 10° is 180°, pointing the opposite way; the true average direction is 0°. Distances between angles must be measured around the circle. So the usual mean, variance and regression can give badly misleading answers for circular data.

Circular statistics

Averaging directions as vectors

Each observation is an angle θ on the unit circle, written as a vector xᵢ = (cos θᵢ, sin θᵢ). The average of these vectors gives the mean resultant vector:

C̄ = (1/n) Σ cos θᵢ,   S̄ = (1/n) Σ sin θᵢ,   θ̄ = tan⁻¹(S̄ / C̄)

In practice the mean direction is computed with the two-argument arctangent, θ̄ = atan2(S̄, C̄). Plain tan⁻¹(S̄/C̄) can't tell opposite directions apart: for directions around 180° it would give an answer near 0°.

Directional distributions

Von Mises (circular normal)

f(θ) = eκ cos(θ − μ) ÷ 2π I₀(κ)

The circle's answer to the normal distribution. μ is the mean direction, κ the concentration (like an inverse variance), and I₀ the modified Bessel function of order 0.

Uniform circular

All directions equally likely: no dominant direction (κ = 0).

Bivariate and spherical

For 3D orientations such as latitude–longitude or azimuth–dip.

Case example: when do customers buy?

A company tracks the time of day of online purchases, converting times to angles (0° = midnight, 180° = noon, so each hour is 15°). The analysis finds a mean purchase direction of 210° and a concentration of R = 0.85: purchases cluster strongly around one time of day.

The slides describe 210° as "≈ 8 PM", but on this scale 210° ÷ 15° = hour 14, which is 2 PM. An 8 PM peak would be 300°. The visual below lets you check the conversion.

Functional data analysis

When each observation is a whole curve

Functional data analysis (FDA) A statistical framework for data that can be viewed as functions, curves or trajectories rather than individual numbers. Each observation is a function, such as a time-dependent curve, instead of a single data point.

In traditional data, an observation is one value, like a person's height. In functional data, it's a curve, like that person's growth curve over time. Many modern sources produce such continuous signals: sensor readings (temperature, ECG, EEG), stock prices through the day, traffic flow, and spectroscopic data (light intensity vs. wavelength).

The core idea

FDA models each observation as a smooth function plus noise, xᵢ(t) = underlying smooth function + noise, where t lies in a continuous interval such as time or space. The functions are represented with a basis expansion:

xᵢ(t) = Σₖ cᵢₖ φₖ(t)

The φₖ(t) are basis functions (B-splines, Fourier terms, wavelets) and the cᵢₖ are each curve's coefficients. A curve with hundreds of noisy readings becomes a handful of coefficients.

Steps in FDA

  1. Smooth the data

    Convert discrete, noisy readings into smooth functions with a basis expansion or kernel smoothing.

  2. Represent each observation as a function

    Of time, space or another continuous domain.

  3. Describe the functions

    Functional analogs of the usual statistics: the mean curve x̄(t) = (1/n) Σ xᵢ(t), variance and covariance functions, functional boxplots and outlier detection.

  4. Functional PCA

    Find the dominant modes of variation among the curves.

  5. Functional regression

    Model relationships where predictors, responses or both are functions.

Regression typeModel
Scalar-on-functionY = α + ∫ X(t) β(t) dt + ε
Function-on-scalarY(t) = α(t) + X β(t) + ε(t)
Function-on-functionY(t) = α(t) + ∫ X(s) β(s, t) ds + ε(t)

Functional clustering and classification group similar curves, such as customers' behavior over time or heartbeat shapes.

Functional PCA

The main ways a set of curves differ

Functional principal component analysis is PCA applied to curves. It decomposes each function as the mean curve plus a weighted sum of components:

xᵢ(t) = x̄(t) + Σₖ ξᵢₖ φₖ(t)

The φₖ(t) are the functional principal components (modes of variation) and the ξᵢₖ are each curve's scores. FPCA identifies dominant patterns such as "overall level", "timing shift" or "peak intensity".

Example: temperature curves for 10 cities

Record daily temperature across 10 cities for a year; each city becomes a smooth curve over 365 days. FDA finds the mean annual temperature curve, and FPCA reveals the main differences: the first component is the overall hotness level, and the second is the contrast between summer and winter extremes.

Captures shape, not just points; handles complex time and space dependencies; reduces dimensions via FPCA; smooths out measurement noise; supports regression, clustering and classification on curves.

Limitations

Session 21 in summary

  • Factor analysis explains correlated variables with a few latent factors: Xᵢ = Σ aᵢⱼFⱼ + eᵢ. Keep factors by eigenvalue or scree plot, rotate for interpretability, then name them.
  • Directional data wraps around, so averages and spreads must be computed on the circle: mean direction θ̄ = atan2(S̄, C̄), resultant length R, circular variance 1 − R.
  • The von Mises distribution is the circular normal; rose diagrams show direction frequencies; the Rayleigh test checks for a preferred direction.
  • Functional data analysis treats each curve as one observation, smoothing noisy readings with basis functions.
  • Functional PCA decomposes curves into a mean plus modes of variation, such as overall level and seasonal amplitude.
