Decision analytics

From data to the best course of action

Decision analytics The process of applying quantitative methods, data and analytical reasoning to guide and improve decision-making under uncertainty. It combines data-driven insight with business context to identify the best course of action among alternatives.

Data collection and processing

Gathering historical, transactional or real-time data.

Modelling and simulation

Mathematical and statistical models of the possible outcomes.

Optimization and decision models

Selecting the best strategy for the objectives.

Evaluation and implementation

Testing, validating and applying the chosen decision.

TypeDescriptionExample
DescriptiveWhat happened, from historical dataMonthly sales reports
PredictiveForecasts future outcomes with statistical modelsPredicting customer churn
PrescriptiveSuggests actions to achieve the desired resultOptimizing supply chain routes

The decision-making framework

  1. Define the problem

    Clarify goals and constraints.

  2. Collect and prepare data

    Ensure accuracy and relevance.

  3. Model the decision

    Decision trees, regression, simulation or optimization.

  4. Analyze alternatives

    Weigh risks, returns and trade-offs.

  5. Decide and implement

    Select the best option and act on it.

  6. Monitor and refine

    Keep assessing performance.

Tools and techniques: decision trees, Monte Carlo simulation, linear and nonlinear optimization, what-if and sensitivity analysis, Bayesian networks, and risk analysis models.

Example: choosing a marketing campaign with a decision tree

A decision tree lays out each alternative, the uncertain outcomes that could follow, their probabilities and payoffs. The expected monetary value (EMV) of an alternative is the probability-weighted average of its payoffs minus its cost; the rational choice under this criterion is the highest EMV, though risk matters too.

Benefits

More accurate and consistent decisions, less bias and uncertainty, better efficiency and profitability, evidence-based justification, and real-time adaptive decisions.

Challenges

Data quality and availability, model complexity and interpretability, changing environments, integration with business processes, and ethical and privacy concerns in automated decisions.

The future

More AI and machine learning for adaptive decisions, IoT and real-time data streams, automated decision systems in business and government, and explainable AI for transparent, ethical decisions.

Decision analytics bridges data and action: it turns raw data into strategic, evidence-based decisions in uncertain, complex environments.

Evaluating classifiers

How good is a model that predicts categories?

Once a classification model (logistic regression, a decision tree, an SVM, a random forest) is built, we must measure how accurately and effectively it predicts the correct class on unseen data. Evaluation shows whether the model generalizes, how many wrong predictions it makes and of which kind, and whether it performs well across all classes, especially in imbalanced datasets. It's how we compare models, detect over- or underfitting, understand the trade-off between false positives and false negatives, and guide tuning.

The confusion matrix

Accuracy

(TP + TN) ÷ (TP + TN + FP + FN)

Share of all predictions that are correct.

Precision (positive predictive value)

TP ÷ (TP + FP)

Of everything flagged positive, how much really is.

Recall (sensitivity, true positive rate)

TP ÷ (TP + FN)

Of all real positives, how many were caught.

Specificity (true negative rate)

TN ÷ (TN + FP)

Of all real negatives, how many were correctly cleared.

F1 score

2 × Precision × Recall ÷ (Precision + Recall)

The harmonic mean: high only when both are high.

The precision–recall trade-off

A classifier usually outputs a score, and a threshold turns it into a yes/no decision. Lowering the threshold catches more positives (higher recall) but raises more false alarms (lower precision), and vice versa. The right balance depends on the business context: medical diagnosis prioritizes recall (don't miss a sick patient), while spam filtering prioritizes precision (don't block a real email).

ROC and precision–recall curves

ROC curve and AUC

The receiver operating characteristic curve plots the true positive rate (recall) against the false positive rate (1 − specificity) at every threshold. The area under it (AUC) summarizes ranking ability: 1.0 is a perfect classifier, 0.5 is random guessing.

Precision–recall curve

Plots precision against recall at every threshold. It is especially useful for imbalanced datasets, where the ROC curve can look reassuring even when most flagged cases are false alarms.

Same model, rarer positives

Interactive

Keep the model the same and change only how common the positive class is. The ROC curve doesn't move, but the precision–recall curve collapses as positives get rarer. Model strength changes both.

For reliable numbers, compute these metrics with k-fold cross-validation rather than a single test split (see session 18), so the evaluation doesn't depend on one particular split.

Real-world example: fraud detection

MetricImportance
AccuracyMisleading: fraud is rare, so "never fraud" scores highly
RecallCrucial: must catch as much fraud as possible
PrecisionImportant: too many false alarms frustrate customers
AUCMeasures how well the model ranks fraud above legitimate transactions

Evaluating a classifier isn't about one metric; it's about choosing the combination that matches the business goals, the class balance, and the cost of each kind of error. Banks prefer high recall with good precision, not just accuracy.

Analytical frameworks

A roadmap from business question to action

Analytical framework A systematic structure that connects business problems, analytical techniques and data-driven insights to enable evidence-based decisions. It defines what data to analyze, how to analyze it, and how to interpret and act on the results.

Its purpose is to organize the process from problem to solution, make analysis consistent and repeatable, align methods with business objectives, and turn data into actionable intelligence.

StepDescriptionChurn example
1. Problem definitionState the business question clearly"Why is churn increasing?"
2. Data collectionIdentify and gather relevant dataCRM data, transactions, feedback
3. Data preparationClean, transform and structureMissing values, outliers, normalization
4. Data analysisStatistical, ML or optimization techniquesRegression, clustering, classification
5. InterpretationTurn results into insightsIdentify churn drivers
6. Decision-makingAct on the insightsDesign a retention campaign
7. Monitoring and feedbackTrack outcomes and refineContinuous improvement

Stages in detail

  1. Define objectives

    Understand what must be achieved, align with organizational goals, and identify KPIs.

  2. Understand and prepare data

    Explore internal and external sources, run exploratory analysis, and clean, transform and engineer features.

  3. Model and analyze

    Pick descriptive (what happened?), predictive (what will happen?) or prescriptive (what should we do?) techniques, build models and validate them.

  4. Interpret and generate insight

    Translate outputs into business meaning, visualize patterns, identify actionable findings.

  5. Decide and implement

    Support management in choosing data-driven actions and build them into strategy.

  6. Evaluate and improve

    Measure the decisions' effectiveness and reassess models as new data arrives.

Evaluation

Did the model, the process and the decision actually work?

Evaluation The systematic assessment of the performance, effectiveness and outcomes of a model, project or analytical solution: how well it meets its objectives, measured with defined metrics and validation techniques. It ensures the analysis is accurate and reliable, useful for decisions, and generalizes to new data.

Its objectives are to verify accuracy and validate models, compare alternatives, ensure alignment with business goals, detect bias or errors, and refine the analytical system. As the final or iterative stage of frameworks like CRISP-DM, it answers: does the model achieve the business goal, is it reliable on unseen data, and should it be deployed, retrained or revised?

The evaluation process

  1. Define success criteria

    Accuracy, revenue, satisfaction, or something else?

  2. Select metrics

    Quantitative (accuracy, MSE) or qualitative (user feedback).

  3. Test with new data

    Holdout or cross-validation data.

  4. Compare models

    Against benchmark models or baselines.

  5. Interpret results

    Translate metrics into business insight.

  6. Refine and repeat

    Tune parameters or update the data.

Evaluation is the bridge between analytics and decision-making. Without evaluation, analysis is just computation; with it, it becomes decision intelligence.

Baseline, performance and investment

Is our data initiative worth it?

Every analytics or AI project must be judged on business value, not just technical accuracy. Three concepts make that possible: the baseline, the performance achieved, and the implications for investment.

Baseline: the starting point

The benchmark before any data-driven change: identify the relevant KPIs, measure current performance with historical or operational data, and keep it as the control for comparison. It's what makes improvement, and ROI, measurable.

Performance: the improvement

How much better the system performs afterwards: analytical performance (accuracy, precision, R²), operational performance (speed, efficiency, cost), and business performance (margins, retention, ROI).

Investment: what to do next

Investment in data means the financial, technical and human resources for collecting, storing, processing, analyzing and governing data. Baseline and performance show whether it's worthwhile: scale up, optimize, or pause.

CategoryExample metricsPurpose
Model performanceAccuracy, F1, AUCPredictive capability
Business KPIsROI, conversion rate, sales growthReal-world impact
Operational efficiencyProcessing time, cost per transactionProcess improvement

Example: a retailer's demand forecast accuracy rose from a baseline of 70% to 88% with predictive analytics. That's a gain of 18 percentage points, or a 26% relative improvement (18 ÷ 70). The slides call it "+18% improvement"; saying "points" avoids confusing the two.

Return on data investment (RODI)

RODI = financial value of the performance improvement ÷ cost of the data initiative

If predictive analytics cut losses by ₹20M and cost ₹5M, RODI = 20 ÷ 5 = 4.0: each ₹1 invested generated ₹4 of value. Note that this ratio counts gross value; the net return, (20 − 5) ÷ 5 = 3.0, is the more common definition of ROI, so be clear which one you report.

What drives the return

  • Data quality: poor data reduces reliability and ROI.
  • Analytical maturity: mature data infrastructure yields bigger gains.
  • Scalability: investments pay off when systems scale across use cases.
  • Talent and culture: skilled teams and a data-driven culture multiply impact.
  • Governance and security: compliance, lower risk and trust.

Continuous monitoring

Evaluate initiatives against baselines regularly and adjust investment as performance changes.

Evidence-based funding

Proven, quantified improvements justify further investment in infrastructure, models and governance.

Session 19 in summary

  • Decision analytics applies data and models to choose among alternatives under uncertainty; decision trees compare options by expected value and risk.
  • Classifiers are judged with the confusion matrix: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1. The threshold trades precision against recall, and the right choice depends on the cost of each error.
  • ROC/AUC measures ranking ability regardless of class balance; precision–recall curves reveal problems with rare positives.
  • Analytical frameworks such as CRISP-DM, OODA and DIKW structure the path from question to action.
  • Evaluation covers models, processes and outcomes, using holdout, cross-validation, bootstrapping and A/B tests.
  • Baseline → performance → investment: measure the starting point, quantify the gain in points and in value, and fund what pays back.
