Population and sample

Studying the part to understand the whole

In statistics, data can come from an entire group or from a smaller part of it. Understanding populations, samples and sampling methods is what makes accurate analysis and inference possible.

Population

The entire group of individuals, items or events you want to study: all patients in a hospital, all students in a school, all products from a factory.

Sample

A subset of the population selected for study, used when studying everyone isn't possible. For example, 100 students surveyed out of 1,000.

A population can be finite, with countable members (1,000 students), or infinite, with uncountably many (atoms in the atmosphere).

Population values and sample estimates

MeasurePopulationSampleExample
Meanμ (mu)x̄ (x-bar)Average height of all 1,000 students vs. the 100 surveyed

Population values are usually unknown. We calculate x̄ and s from a sample and use them to estimate μ and σ. How good that estimate is depends on how the sample was chosen.

Univariate and bivariate sampling

One variable at a time, or two together

Univariate sampling

Sampling or studying one variable at a time to describe or summarize one feature, such as students' height or patients' blood pressure. Tools: mean, median, mode, variance, histogram.

Bivariate sampling

Recording two variables for each observation to find relationships between them, such as height and weight, or age and wound healing time. Tools: correlation coefficient (r), regression, scatter plot.

Resampling

Reusing the data you have to judge how far to trust it

Resampling A technique that repeatedly draws samples from existing data to assess variation or accuracy. It estimates the precision of sample statistics, improves model reliability, and tests hypotheses.
MethodHow it worksUsed to

Sample spaces and events

Listing what can happen, then picking out what we care about

Understanding sample spaces and events is the first step in analyzing random experiments. Together they define all possible outcomes and pick out specific occurrences within them.

Random experiment

An experiment whose outcome can't be predicted with certainty: tossing a coin, rolling a die, drawing a card from a deck.

Sample space (S)

The set of all possible outcomes.

One coin: S = {H, T}
Two coins: S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}
One die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Event (E)

A subset of the sample space: one or more outcomes, written E ⊆ S.

Even number on a die: E = {2, 4, 6}
Heads on a coin: E = {H}

Event operations

Union E₁ ∪ E₂

Either E₁ or E₂ (or both) happens.

P(E₁ ∪ E₂) = P(E₁) + P(E₂) − P(E₁ ∩ E₂)

Intersection E₁ ∩ E₂

Both E₁ and E₂ happen together.

Complement E′

E does not happen.

P(E′) = 1 − P(E)

Difference E₁ − E₂

E₁ happens but E₂ does not.

Probability of an event

Measuring how likely an event is, from 0 to 1

P(E) = number of favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes in the sample space

For example, P(even number on a die) = 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5. This formula assumes every outcome is equally likely.

The three axioms of probability

Every probability rule in this session follows from three basic axioms.

1. Non-negativity

P(E) ≥ 0

A probability can never be negative. The smallest possible value is 0, an impossible event.

2. Normalization

P(S) = 1

Something in the sample space must happen, so all probabilities lie between 0 and 1. It also gives the complement rule: P(E′) = 1 − P(E).

3. Additivity

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

When A and B are mutually exclusive. If they overlap, subtract P(A ∩ B) so the overlap isn't counted twice.

Try the axioms in the die explorer above: the union rule always subtracts the overlap, and A plus its complement always sums to 1.

Joint, marginal and conditional probability

Working with more than one event at a time

Real problems usually involve more than one variable or event. Take two events about a student: A, the student passes math, and B, the student passes science. Three kinds of probability describe them.

Joint probability

P(A ∩ B)

Both events happen together. Outcomes common to A and B, divided by total outcomes. If P(A ∩ B) = 0.3, 30% of students passed both subjects.

Marginal probability

P(A) = ΣB P(A ∩ B)

One event on its own, regardless of the others. Found by adding up the joint probabilities that involve A: the probability of passing math, whatever the science result.

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B)

One event, given that another has already happened (P(B) must be above 0). If a student passed science, there's a 0.3 ÷ 0.5 = 60% chance they also passed math.

How they connect

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B) = P(B | A) × P(A)

This relationship lets you convert between joint, conditional and marginal probabilities, and it is exactly where Bayes' theorem comes from.

Independence

Events A and B are independent if knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

If the two sides aren't equal, the events are dependent. In the student table, P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.3. Since 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3, passing math and passing science are independent here, which is why P(A | B) equals P(A).

Real-world example

Let A be "a patient has diabetes" and B be "a patient has high blood pressure". Suppose P(A) = 0.25, P(B) = 0.30 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.15. Then:

P(A | B) = 0.15 ÷ 0.30 = 0.5

Half of the patients with high blood pressure also have diabetes, twice the 25% rate across all patients, so the two conditions are dependent. (These values are loaded in the Venn diagram above.)

Bayes' theorem

Updating a belief when new evidence arrives

Bayes' theorem describes how to update the probability of a hypothesis when new evidence arrives. It links two conditional probabilities: the probability of A given B, and the probability of B given A.

P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A)P(B)
Posterior P(A | B)What we believe now, after seeing the evidence.
Likelihood P(B | A)How likely the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
Prior P(A)What we believed before seeing the evidence.
Evidence P(B)How likely the evidence is overall, across all hypotheses.

It comes straight from the relationship above: since P(A | B) × P(B) = P(B | A) × P(A), dividing both sides by P(B) gives the theorem. The evidence term is found with the law of total probability:

P(B) = P(B | A) × P(A) + P(B | A′) × P(A′)

Session 6 in summary

  • A sample stands in for the population: x̄ and s estimate μ and σ, and random sampling keeps those estimates unbiased.
  • Univariate analysis describes one variable; bivariate analysis looks for relationships between two.
  • Resampling (bootstrap, jackknife, cross-validation) measures how reliable a statistic or model is.
  • A sample space lists every outcome; events are subsets of it, combined by union, intersection and complement.
  • Joint, marginal and conditional probabilities are linked by P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B).
  • Bayes' theorem turns a prior into a posterior using evidence, and a rare condition stays fairly unlikely even after a positive result from a good test.
